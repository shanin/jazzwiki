# 3 Iconic Clavinet Grooves

**Presenter:** Adam Maness | **Channel:** Open Studio
**Duration:** 37:28 | **YouTube:** [▶ Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=RERb9-QChNs)

## Overview

Adam Maness guides a deep dive into three foundational funk-soul clavinet grooves: Superstition by Stevie Wonder, Use Me by Bill Withers, and Mothership Connection by Parliament. Rather than learning the grooves in isolation, the focus is on pocket practice—learning to stay locked into the rhythm section and surrender to the groove over extended loops. This is essential discipline for pianists and keyboardists who typically have more harmonic freedom in straight-ahead jazz but must serve the music first in funk contexts.

## Tunes & Standards Referenced

- *[[Superstition]]* (Stevie Wonder) — the foundational clavinet groove, played on Soul Train; focus on filling space with ghost notes and staying in pocket
- *[[Use Me]]* (Bill Withers) — Ray Jackson on clavinet; simple but deceptively challenging two-chord (Em7 to A7) riff in octaves
- *[[Mothership Connection]]* (Parliament) — a funky riff that loops for extended periods in the live recording; staying in this pocket can be transformative

## Musicians & Influences Referenced

- [[Stevie Wonder]] — composer and performer of Superstition; playing clavinet on Soul Train with intricate ghost note fills
- [[Bill Withers]] — singer and composer; features Ray Jackson on clavinet for this funk classic
- [[Ray Jackson]] — clavinet player on the Use Me recording; iconic simple yet effective riff
- [[Bernie Worrell]] — legendary keys player for Parliament; plays clavinet and synths on Mothership Connection
- [[Chick Corea]] — referenced for the concept of the "10 drummers" that keyboardists can deploy to support the rhythm section

## Concepts Introduced

- [[Pocket]] — extended looping of a single groove to develop comfort staying in the pocket without adding unnecessary fills or improvisation
- [[Clavinet]] — keyboard instrument used prominently in funk and soul; has a distinctive woody, percussive sound
- Ghost Notes — subtle off-beat fills played quietly to add texture and human feel without disrupting the main groove
- Rhythm Section Surrender — letting go of typical jazz piano freedom and serving the groove and band as part of the furnace that makes people dance
- Groove Anticipation — listening to the original recording and noting where the performer anticipates or plays slightly ahead/behind the beat

## Practice Notes

The instructor emphasizes several key principles:
- Loop each groove for an "uncomfortably long" amount of time to train the ear and body to resist the urge to improvise or fill space early.
- Listen for anticipation and micro-rhythmic variations in the original recordings—the human element that makes grooves feel alive, but only add your own voice once you've truly locked in the pocket.
- Use the PDF guide (downloadable in description) as a reference, but prioritize listening and playing by ear.
- Notice how the original musicians (Stevie, Ray Jackson, Bernie Worrell) maintain the groove while adding their own voice—a balance between serving the music and speaking your truth.

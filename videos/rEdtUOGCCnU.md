# Why Do I Still Suck?

**Presenter:** Adam Maness | **Channel:** Open Studio
**Duration:** 22:15 | **YouTube:** [▶ Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rEdtUOGCCnU)

## Overview

Despite years of practice and knowledge of scales, voicings, and many standards, many jazz musicians struggle with improvisation. This lesson addresses the core issue: musicians often focus on what notes to play rather than how to play them. The instructor demonstrates that strong rhythmic vocabulary—swing, syncopation, and feel—matters far more than harmonic knowledge. Through the lens of Thelonious Monk's compositions, the video teaches students how to internalize rhythmic language and apply it to their own improvisation.

## Tunes & Standards Referenced

- *[[Straight No Chaser]]* (Thelonious Monk) — used as a template for learning advanced syncopated rhythmic patterns
- *Rhythm Changes* — blues and jazz standard progression used as accompaniment for rhythmic vocabulary exercises
- *B-flat Blues* — blues standard used to practice rhythmic phrasing divorced from harmonic vocabulary

## Musicians & Influences Referenced

- [[Buddy Bolden]] — introduced as originator of the "big four" rhythmic concept foundational to jazz
- [[Thelonious Monk]] — primary composer and model for rhythmic vocabulary; two of his compositions serve as the basis for all exercises
- [[Winton Marsalis]] — featured in Ken Burns' jazz documentary explaining the importance of the big four concept
- [[Clark Terry]] — referenced as an example of conversational, rhythmic phrasing

## Concepts Introduced

- [[Rhythmic Vocabulary]] — one of the most important fundamentals of jazz improvisation, often overlooked in favor of harmonic study
- [[Syncopation]] — the practice of accenting beats outside the regular pulse, central to jazz's rhythmic language
- [[Swing]] — the forward-momentum feel achieved through specific rhythmic placements and confidence
- [[The Big Four]] — the signature rhythmic accent pattern originating in New Orleans, key to understanding jazz's historical roots
- [[Anticipation of One]] — a specific syncopation technique where phrases begin ahead of the downbeat, creating momentum

## Practice Notes

The video emphasizes felt learning over analytical understanding. Students are encouraged to sing rhythmic patterns before playing them, to internalize the feel rather than calculate it. The exercises use limited note choices (four notes on rhythm changes; all notes on the blues) to prove that rhythm matters more than harmonic accuracy. Confidence and swagger are essential—approaching the rhythm with uncertainty undermines the effect.

# 7 SICK Chord Substitutions

**Presenter:** Adam Maness | **Channel:** Open Studio
**Duration:** 21:58 | **YouTube:** [▶ Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hWCX9-DnMG0)

## Overview

This lesson explores seven different ways to substitute chords in the ubiquitous [[3-6-2-5 turnaround]], specifically focusing on substitutions for the 6 chord. By understanding chord color, root movement, and tension control, musicians can add sophistication and personal voice to one of jazz's most common harmonic progressions. The instructor demonstrates each substitution with voicing principles based on [[1-3-5-7 voicing]] and the [[hinging]] technique.

## Tunes & Standards Referenced

None directly named in this lesson.

## Musicians & Influences Referenced

- [[Barry Harris]] — referenced regarding the [[Flat 3 diminished]] as an underexplored but beautiful harmonic device

## Concepts Introduced

- [[3-6-2-5 turnaround]] — foundational harmonic progression and its substitution possibilities
- [[Secondary dominance]] — first substitution method using dominant 7 chords
- [[Tritone substitution]] — dominant chord a tritone (half step above) the target
- [[1-3-5-7 voicing]] — foundational framework for building all voicings in this lesson
- [[Hinging]] — technique of extending or altering one note of the 1-3-5-7 structure to add color
- [[Parallel minor from above]] — minor 7 chord a half step above the target
- [[Flat 3 diminished]] — diminished chord built a half step above the target
- [[Moo chord]] — modern-sounding voicing combining a triad over an upper third
- [[Parallel minor from below]] — minor 7 chord approached from a tritone below the target

## Practice Notes

All voicings in this lesson are built from [[1-3-5-7 voicing]] structures with [[hinging]] off the root, third, and fifth. The bass note is often detached from the harmonic voicing. Focus on how each substitution changes the color, root movement, and tension of the progression rather than specific note names or fingerings. The lesson emphasizes that these seven substitutions work on any chord, not just the 6 chord in the [[3-6-2-5 turnaround]].

Listeners' ears crave a mix between the familiar and the unfamiliar, so great players know when to throw in unexpected harmonic changes. Understanding the different substitution options even for just one chord in the progression can be game-changing for how one approaches a tune.

### [3:09] Understanding the 3-6-2-5 — [▶](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hWCX9-DnMG0&t=189s)

[3:09](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hWCX9-DnMG0&t=189s) In any given key center, diatonic chords are built off the major scale by skipping one note. In F major, the 1 chord is F major 7, the 2 chord is G minor 7, the 3 chord is A minor 7, the 4 chord is B flat major 7, the 5 chord is C7, the 6 chord is D minor 7 (the relative minor), and the 7 chord is E minor 7 flat 5. A [[3-6-2-5 turnaround]] progression denotes chords that usually end up in a 1 or vamp back and forth. In the key of F, this progression is A minor 7, D minor 7, G minor 7, C7, and resolving to F. The goal of this lesson is to explore substitutions for the 6 chord specifically, which is the D minor 7 in this example.

### [5:17] Substitution 1: Diatonic 3-6-2-5 — [▶](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hWCX9-DnMG0&t=317s)

[5:17](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hWCX9-DnMG0&t=317s) The first substitution is simply the regular diatonic [[3-6-2-5 turnaround]] progression using only chords from the key. This serves as the baseline: A minor 7, D minor 7, G minor 7, and C7. Though the lesson will use different Roman numerals as substitutions are explored, these numerals will remain the same to emphasize that musicians have the freedom to change any chord to anything that sounds good whenever they want. The turnarounds are malleable and can be adjusted to fit the tension desired.

### [7:03] Substitution 2: Secondary Dominant — [▶](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hWCX9-DnMG0&t=423s)

[7:03](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hWCX9-DnMG0&t=423s) The second substitution replaces the D minor 7 with a D7 dominant chord, which is called a [[secondary dominance]]. This creates tonicization, where a dominant chord is placed a fourth below or fifth above the target chord (in this case, G minor 7) to give it more weight and leading power. Secondary dominants add a bit more tension than the diatonic approach. A practical advantage is that musicians do not need permission to make this substitution; if the feeling is right and it works with the melody or soloist, a D7 can be substituted for D minor 7.

### [8:59] Voicing Framework: 1-3-5-7 and Hinging — [▶](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hWCX9-DnMG0&t=539s)

[8:59](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hWCX9-DnMG0&t=539s) The instructor describes the harmonic approach used throughout the lesson: all chords are built from a [[1-3-5-7 voicing]] structure and do not rely on scales. Instead of using a particular scale to generate alterations, voicings are constructed by [[hinging]] from different points of the 1-3-5-7. The root can hinge up to any natural, flat, or sharp 9. The fifth can hinge down to a flat 5 or up to a 13. The third can hinge up to a fourth (creating a suspended third). This approach keeps the 1-3-5-7 always represented and creates natural voice leading and tension. The bass note is often kept separate from the 1-3-5-7 harmonic structure.

### [11:19] Substitution 3: Tritone Substitution — [▶](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hWCX9-DnMG0&t=679s)

[11:19](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hWCX9-DnMG0&t=679s) The [[tritone substitution]] replaces the secondary dominant (D7) with an Ab7 chord. The tritone is the interval a half step above D, and Ab7 can approach G minor 7 (the target chord). An easy way to remember this is that tritone subs are always a half step above their target. When approaching any diatonic seventh chord, approaching it from a half step above with a dominant chord always sounds good. This substitution is particularly slick because the 1-3-5-7 of Ab7 is fully represented in the voicing.

### [13:20] Substitution 4: Parallel Minor from Above — [▶](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hWCX9-DnMG0&t=800s)

[13:20](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hWCX9-DnMG0&t=800s) This substitution is even simpler than the tritone sub because it is not a different kind of chord. The [[Parallel minor from above]] involves taking the target minor 7 chord and approaching it from a half step above using the same chord type. For G minor 7, this means approaching it with Ab minor 7. This substitution is very slick and can be used whenever one is approaching a minor 7 chord. The license to use this substitution exists because of the harmonic tension and beauty it creates.

### [15:00] Substitution 5: Flat 3 Diminished — [▶](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hWCX9-DnMG0&t=900s)

[15:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hWCX9-DnMG0&t=900s) The [[Flat 3 diminished]] (also called the tonic diminished) is the instructor's favorite substitution. It is a diminished chord built a flat 3 above the scale degree being substituted. For the 6 chord substitution, a diminished chord is placed a half step above the target. This creates beautiful inner voice movement and is described as one of the most enjoyable things to play on the piano. The [[Flat 3 diminished]] is notably underexplored in modern jazz practice, a situation the instructor and [[Barry Harris]] find surprising and lamentable. A dedicated video on the tonic diminished is planned.

### [16:45] Substitution 6: Moo Chord — [▶](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hWCX9-DnMG0&t=1005s)

[16:45](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hWCX9-DnMG0&t=1005s) The [[Moo chord]] is a modern-sounding substitution that works particularly well for listeners familiar with Steely Dan's harmonic language. This voicing is a cousin of the [[secondary dominance]] and is constructed as a D add 2 triad over F sharp (over the third of D major). This voicing creates beautiful half-step movement into the next chord and is built entirely on [[1-3-5-7 voicing]] hinging principles. The bass is attached to this voicing, and the specific notes are D, E, F sharp, and A.

### [19:26] Substitution 7: Parallel Minor from Below — [▶](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hWCX9-DnMG0&t=1166s)

[19:26](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hWCX9-DnMG0&t=1166s) The final substitution is the [[Parallel minor from below]], which approaches the target chord from a tritone below. For G minor 7, this uses a chord built on F sharp (a tritone below G), creating one of the most beautiful sounds in the lesson. If playing with a bass player or singer, they might need very big ears to accept this substitution without warning, but for solo piano or trio contexts, the sound is gorgeous. This completes the seven substitution methods.

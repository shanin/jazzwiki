# 4 Basic Chord Voicings Every Jazz Musician Should Know

**Presenter:** Adam Maness | **Channel:** Open Studio
**Duration:** 10:26 | **YouTube:** [▶ Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=orCNZHqTX90)

## Overview

Adam Maness presents four foundational chord voicing techniques essential for all jazz musicians, regardless of instrument. These voicings form the structural basis for jazz harmony and arrangement. Understanding these techniques enables musicians to voice chords effectively whether playing piano, arranging for ensemble, or supporting other musicians.

## Tunes & Standards Referenced

None explicitly referenced by title in this lesson.

## Musicians & Influences Referenced

- [[Barry Harris]] — referenced for the sixth diminished scale framework

## Concepts Introduced

- [[Root Shell Pretty]] — systematic three-layer approach: root at the bottom, shell (third/seventh) in the middle, pretty notes on top
- [[Closed Voicings]] — five-note block chords with octaves spread between hands
- [[Drop Two]] — taking the second-from-top note of a closed voicing and dropping it down an octave
- [[Fourths Voicing]] — voicings constructed literally from fourth intervals
- [[So-What Voicing]] — fourths with a third on top, easily transposed diatonically
- [[Spread Voicing]] — left-hand voicing with octaves in the right hand and extensions in the middle

## Practice Notes

The lesson emphasizes that all four voicing types should be learned in all 12 keys. The Root Shell Pretty framework is particularly valuable because it makes the logic of voicing transparent: once you understand the three-layer structure, the same principle applies to any chord quality or inversion. Closed voicings and their drop variations (particularly drop two) are essential for creating voice leading across chord progressions. Fourths and so-what voicings unlock modal harmony and diatonic movement. Spread voicings are particularly useful when arranging for larger ensembles.

### [00:58] Rooted Voicing: Root Shell Pretty — [▶](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=orCNZHqTX90&t=58s)

[01:01](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=orCNZHqTX90&t=61s) The first voicing type is the [[Root Shell Pretty]] voicing, also called 2+3, 3+2, or 3+3 depending on how notes are distributed between the hands. The concept is consistent regardless of the specific hand assignment: these are rooted two-handed voicings built from three layers. The first layer is the root, which anchors the voicing at the bottom. The second layer is the shell—the third and seventh of the chord, which defines whether the chord is major, minor, dominant, or half-diminished. Nothing goes between the root and shell; they form an unbroken foundation. The third layer is the pretty notes—upper extensions like the ninth, eleventh, or thirteenth, and any alterations used on dominant chords.

[02:18](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=orCNZHqTX90&t=138s) Demonstrating this on an F minor 7 chord in a 3+3 split: the left hand plays the root (F), the seventh (E-flat), and the third (A-flat as a tenth). The right hand then adds color notes—the eleventh (B-flat), the thirteenth (D), and the ninth on top. This transparent structure shows how extensions layer on top of the harmonic foundation, which is why the framework is valuable for musicians of all instruments.

[02:55](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=orCNZHqTX90&t=175s) The same principle applies to the next chord in a ii-V-I progression. On B-flat 7 (the V chord), the left hand again plays root, third, and seventh with no doubling. The right hand adds altered extensions: flat-9, sharp-11, and natural-13. Critically, no notes are doubled between the hands, and the third is avoided in doubling for overtone reasons.

[03:45](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=orCNZHqTX90&t=225s) The I chord (E-flat major 7) uses a 6-9 voicing structure: root and third in the left hand, with the sixth and ninth in the right hand. When these three chords are played together, the voice leading flows smoothly. The fundamental architecture remains constant: root and shell form the foundation, and everything above that is a pretty note—whether it is the ninth, eleventh, thirteenth, fifth, or any altered extension. This basic structure can be applied to any chord quality.

### [04:59] Block Chords & Closed Voicings — [▶](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=orCNZHqTX90&t=299s)

[04:59](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=orCNZHqTX90&t=299s) The second voicing type is block chords, also called [[Closed Voicings]] or lock hands. These are five-note voicings where the left hand plays a single note (the octave of the top note of the chord), and the right hand fills in the chord. The scale used does not matter for this structure; what matters is the octave relationship.

[05:24](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=orCNZHqTX90&t=324s) Demonstrating C major 6: the chord is C, E, G, A, C. The next example is C major 7 with the 7 on top: B, C, E, G, B. In these five-note voicings, the shell (third and seventh) and at least one color note are always present. Octaves spread between the hands is the defining structural rule. Variants on closed voicings include [[Drop Two]] voicings, where the second note from the top is dropped down an octave. The drop-two variant is the most common application of this structure.

[05:50](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=orCNZHqTX90&t=350s) Adam mentions the six diminished approach, referencing [[Barry Harris]]'s framework as something to explore further, which works particularly well with closed voicings.

[06:33](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=orCNZHqTX90&t=393s) Block chords can also be clustered or voiced with flexibility, but the octave relationship remains the essential rule. The drop-two variant is the most widely used and is a fundamental closed voicing technique.

### [06:38] Fourths Voicings & So-What Voicings — [▶](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=orCNZHqTX90&t=398s)

[06:38](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=orCNZHqTX90&t=398s) The third voicing type is [[Fourths Voicing]] and the related [[So-What Voicing]] voicings. Fourths voicings are voicings literally built in successive fourth intervals. They can be four-note, five-note, or six-note structures. On a dominant chord, fourths can be built diatonically; for example, an F7 built from the seventh creates a diatonic fourth voicing.

[07:06](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=orCNZHqTX90&t=426s) A so-what voicing is a fourth voicing topped with a major third (or sometimes a minor third). What makes fourths voicings crucial is that they are easily moved around diatonically. In D minor with a so-what voicing using four fourths and a major third on top, the voicing uses only white keys on the piano and can be moved up diatonically to fit other keys without modification. Pianists often overuse this technique because of how easily it transposes.

[07:58](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=orCNZHqTX90&t=478s) Similarly, straight-up fourth voicings moved diatonically generate interesting intervallic content. When a fourth voicing contains a tritone interval on top, the sound becomes particularly striking. Fourths voicings and so-what voicings are crucial for understanding modal harmony and creating a strong, chordal sound.

### [08:15] Spread Voicings — [▶](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=orCNZHqTX90&t=495s)

[08:15](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=orCNZHqTX90&t=495s) The fourth and final voicing type is [[Spread Voicing]]. The only structural rule is that the left hand plays any basic voicing (a block chord, cluster, or fourth voicing), and the right hand plays octaves with a note in the middle—typically a fourth or fifth up from the bottom note of the octave, creating a spread effect.

[08:39](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=orCNZHqTX90&t=519s) Demonstrating an A-flat major 9: the left hand plays a block chord (A-flat major 7), and the right hand plays the ninth, thirteenth, and another ninth in a spread pattern across octaves. The same structure works for other chords; an F minor 7 can be voiced this way, and a B-flat 7 can use a shell in the left hand with altered extensions (flat-13, flat-9, sharp-11, flat-9) spread across octaves in the right hand.

[09:20](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=orCNZHqTX90&t=560s) Spread voicings can be moved up and down the keyboard while keeping the left-hand voicing stable. These voicings are particularly useful when arranging for larger ensembles, as they create thick, full sounds that cover a lot of harmonic ground. When a pianist executes spread voicings, other ensemble members should step back, as the sound becomes substantial and all-encompassing.

# Crooked Face Chords

**Presenter:** Adam Maness | **Channel:** Open Studio
**Duration:** 10:19 | **YouTube:** [▶ Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=q-Wb0CsXV6U)

## Overview

This lesson demonstrates three fundamental chord substitution techniques—secondary dominance, tritone substitution, and the Open Studio Moo chord—that transform basic, boring chord progressions into sophisticated, expressive sequences. The video uses a simple I-vi-IV-V progression in C major as a canvas, showing how each technique independently (and in combination) adds harmonic richness and "crookedness" that engages listeners emotionally.

## Tunes & Standards Referenced

- *Steely Dan compositions* — reference to the Mu chord (E major triad add 9), which appears in Steely Dan's work and is adapted as the Open Studio Moo voicing

## Musicians & Influences Referenced

- [[Peter Martin]] — jazz pianist whose "favorite" chords (cush chords / modal interchange) are demonstrated at the end of the video
- [[Donald Fagan]] — co-founder of Steely Dan; his specific definition of the Mu chord (which requires the 9 and 3 together) is referenced and deliberately reinterpreted as the Open Studio Moo

## Concepts Introduced

- [[Secondary dominance]] — V chords built a fifth above any target diatonic chord in a progression, creating sophisticated harmonic movement
- [[Tritone substitution]] — dominant chords positioned a half step above the target chord instead of a traditional V-I relationship
- [[Moo chord]] — a modern voicing technique combining a major triad with an added ninth, voiced with the third in the bass
- [[Modal interchange]] (cush chords) — borrowed chords from parallel minor keys, creating sophisticated chord colors
- Upper-structure voicings and harmonic color techniques

## Practice Notes

The instructor emphasizes that the core progression (C-Am-F-G) remains constant throughout all demonstrations, allowing listeners to hear exactly how each technique transforms the same underlying harmony. Each technique can be applied independently or combined with others. The key insight is that these are not arbitrary embellishments but systematic, learnable approaches to harmonic enrichment.

### [4:29] Tritone Substitution — [▶](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=q-Wb0CsXV6U&t=269s)

[4:29](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=q-Wb0CsXV6U&t=269s) [[Tritone substitution]] is introduced as an alternative approach to secondary dominants. Rather than using E7 to lead to Am, the root is changed a tritone away (from E to B flat), creating a B flat 7 sharp 11. The third and seventh of the original dominant remain, giving the voicing a "technical" yet beautiful quality.

[5:05](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=q-Wb0CsXV6U&t=305s) The instructor simplifies the concept: instead of thinking in terms of tritone displacement and altered extensions, the key insight is that each landing chord (C, Am, F, G) can be approached from a half step above. This approach—using sus chords instead of sharp 11s for tritone substitutions—creates an alternative voicing texture that still delivers harmonic sophistication.

### [6:11] Open Studio Moo Chord — [▶](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=q-Wb0CsXV6U&t=371s)

[6:11](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=q-Wb0CsXV6U&t=371s) The final primary technique is the [[Moo chord]], introduced as a voicing related to the Steely Dan Mu chord. A Mu chord is defined as a major triad with an added ninth (e.g., E major add 9: E, G#, B, F#). The Open Studio Moo is a modification: the third of the chord (G sharp) is placed in the bass, creating E major add 9 / G sharp.

[6:53](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=q-Wb0CsXV6U&t=413s) The instructor notes that [[Donald Fagan]] (Steely Dan) specifies that a true Mu chord cannot separate the nine and third—they must be together. By placing the third in the bass, the Open Studio variation technically breaks this definition, hence the playful name "Open Studio Moo" rather than "Mu." The instructor humorously states, "If they get to make stuff up, so do we."

[7:43](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=q-Wb0CsXV6U&t=463s) The Moo chord is demonstrated as a transition between C and A in the progression, and the instructor emphasizes that this voicing technique has been used by church musicians long before Steely Dan formalized it. The voicing is particularly clean and works across multiple inversions.

### [8:15] Modal Interchange (Cush Chords) — [▶](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=q-Wb0CsXV6U&t=495s)

[8:15](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=q-Wb0CsXV6U&t=495s) The final substitution paradigm introduced is [[Modal interchange]], playfully called "cush chords" in the video. Rather than embellishing the progression using dominant approaches or tritone subs, this technique borrows chords from the parallel minor key (C minor). The basic progression (1-6-4-5 in C major: C, Am, F, G) is transformed by taking the 6, 4, and 5 up a minor third into the key of E flat major, while keeping the tonic (C) in the original key.

[8:20](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=q-Wb0CsXV6U&t=500s) The resulting progression becomes C major, C minor, A flat major 7, B flat 7—all while the tonic C remains constant. This creates a sophisticated, flowing harmonic progression. The instructor notes that secondary dominance, Moo chords, and tritone subs can even be layered onto this modal interchange framework for additional richness.

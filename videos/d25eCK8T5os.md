# The Greatest Theory Lesson of All Time

**Presenter:** Adam Maness | **Channel:** Open Studio
**Duration:** 28:31 | **YouTube:** [▶ Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=d25eCK8T5os)

## Overview

This lesson analyzes Charlie Parker's composition Donnelly as the definitive music theory lesson in jazz. Through detailed transcription and harmonic breakdown, the instructor reveals how Parker answers fundamental questions about scale choices over secondary dominant chords, voicing structures, and chromatic passage work. The lesson demonstrates that the masters have already solved our musical problems; the work is to extract their solutions from the recordings they left behind.

## Tunes & Standards Referenced

- *[[Donnelly]]* — Charlie Parker's composition analyzed as a complete theory lesson on secondary dominants, harmonic minor scales, and broken ninth chords
- *Rhythm Changes* — referenced as an example of how secondary dominants appear in a standard form (the vi chord played as a dominant)

## Musicians & Influences Referenced

- [[Charlie Parker]] — composer and performer whose Donnelly recording (with Miles Davis from the Savoy Dial sessions) serves as the model for this analysis
- [[Miles Davis]] — co-performer on the Donnelly recording examined in this lesson
- [[Barry Harris]] — innovator in chromatic scale techniques; his chromatic passages are referenced as appearing in Donnelly

## Concepts Introduced

- [[Secondary dominance]] — the use of dominant seventh chords that resolve to chords other than the tonic
- [[Harmonic minor scale]] — the parent scale for building scale options over secondary dominant chords
- [[Broken ninth chords]] — the structure and voicing of arpeggiated ninth chords used throughout Donnelly

## Practice Notes

The video uses Sound Slice notation during the initial listen-through. Learning is progressive: first at 50% speed, then incrementally at 60%, 70%, 80%, 90%, and finally full tempo. The instructor emphasizes that mastering the phrasing and rhythmic placement (not just note accuracy) is essential to capturing Parker's intent. The lesson focuses on the melody head, not the solo, as it contains the essential theory concepts in distilled form.

[3:57](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=d25eCK8T5os&t=241s) In the key of A-flat, the tune moves to an F7 chord, converting the natural vi chord (F minor 7) into a dominant seventh. ### [5:41] The Secret Dominant Scale — [▶](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=d25eCK8T5os&t=341s)

[5:41](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=d25eCK8T5os&t=341s) The scale Parker employs is based on the [[Harmonic minor scale]] of the resolution target. Since the F7 chord resolves to B-flat (either major or minor), the B-flat harmonic minor scale provides the parent scale. Starting the B-flat harmonic minor from F yields a powerful set of color tones for the dominant chord. Additionally, Parker includes a sharp nine (the note A-flat), which creates what is informally called the secret dominant scale. This scale contains the flat nine, sharp nine, and major third all together, grounding the listener firmly in the minor key the dominant resolves toward. The scale is far more evocative than Mixolydian when approaching a minor chord resolution, as it suggests the darker harmonic color ahead.

### [5:54] The Secret Dominant Scale in Action — [▶](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=d25eCK8T5os&t=354s)

[5:54](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=d25eCK8T5os&t=354s) The utility of this scale is remarkable on any dominant seventh chord, especially when resolving to a minor or secondary dominant situation. Musicians can either use it as a harmonic minor scale (omitting the sharp nine) or include the sharp nine for extra hip color. The motion from sharp nine down through the flat nine to the root and then to the major third of the target is musically satisfying and provides clarity about the harmonic direction.

### [6:06] Broken Ninth Chords — [▶](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=d25eCK8T5os&t=369s)

[6:06](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=d25eCK8T5os&t=369s) The second key concept is the [[Broken ninth chords]] structure. A ninth voicing contains the third, fifth, seventh, and ninth of a chord. Rather than playing these notes in a close stack, Parker breaks them across the range, often placing the third an octave higher. This approach creates a specific phrase shape that appears throughout Donnelly and is fundamental to the bebop language. Broken ninth chords efficiently outline harmonic changes and are ubiquitous in post-bebop jazz as well.

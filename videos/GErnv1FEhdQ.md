# How To Sound Like Red Garland

**Presenter:** Adam Maness | **Channel:** Open Studio
**Duration:** 20:25 | **YouTube:** [▶ Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=GErnv1FEhdQ)

## Overview

This guided practice session focuses on a specific harmonic technique used by pianist Red Garland on a famous recording from Miles Davis's landmark album. The instructor breaks down the voicing approach, explains the unusual interval relationships that make it distinctive, and leads students through a structured practice progression to master the technique at tempo.

## Tunes & Standards Referenced

- *[[You're My Everything]]* — the ballad featured in the original recording, played with the Miles Davis Quintet in the studio false start and full take
- *Relaxin' with the Miles Davis Quintet* — the 1958 Prestige Records album containing the target recording; contextualizes the production history of Davis's contract completion work

## Musicians & Influences Referenced

- [[Miles Davis]] — bandleader and trumpeter of the Prestige Quintet; directs Red Garland to play block chords during the studio false start
- [[Red Garland]] — pianist and comping specialist; primary focus of the lesson, demonstrating the voicing technique in question
- [[Ethan Iverson]] — pianist and blogger; referenced for his writing on Red's technique in a post called "Red's Bells"

## Concepts Introduced

- Block chords — stacked voicing technique employing intervals that violate traditional voice-leading rules, creating a distinctive sound
- Spread voicings — two-hand arrangement where left hand anchors the block chord foundation and right hand plays octaves with an interior perfect fifth

## Practice Notes

The lesson uses a structured progression: single hands separately (right hand melody only, left hand block chords only), then both hands together at a slower tempo (around quarter note = 60), before gradual tempo increase to match the original recording. Pedal technique is emphasized throughout: lift and replace the sustain pedal just before each strike to maintain harmonic clarity. The practice passages use the same voicing shapes transposed across the 12 keys.

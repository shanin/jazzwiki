# The Easy Way To Get Good At Music

**Presenter:** Adam Maness | **Channel:** Open Studio
**Duration:** 11:16 | **YouTube:** [▶ Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=oRdkepq3ARk)

## Overview

This lesson reframes transcription—learning solos and passages by ear from recordings—as the foundational practice for developing musicianship in jazz. Rather than viewing it as an intimidating, optional activity, Maness presents it as the accessible path to genuine improvement, grounded in the methods and philosophies of working musicians and educators like [[Christian McBride]], [[Robert Glasper]], and others featured here.

## Tunes & Standards Referenced

- *[[Billy's Bounce]]* ([[Charlie Parker]]) — recommended transcription for beginners
- *[[Sandu]]* (Clifford Brown) — intermediate transcription challenge
- *[[Freddy Freeloader]]* (Miles Davis) — intermediate transcription, features Wynton Kelly solo

## Musicians & Influences Referenced

- Adam Maness — instructor and course curator at Open Studio
- [[Christian McBride]] — bassist and educator discussing the primacy of feel over notation
- [[Robert Glasper]] — contemporary pianist on learning music by ear rather than from books
- [[Charlie Parker]] — bebop saxophonist, example of musician who transcribed deeply
- [[Lester Young]] — tenor saxophonist referenced for his musical priorities
- Clifford Brown — hard bop trumpet player whose solo on Sandu is recommended
- Wynton Kelly — pianist whose Freddy Freeloader solo is highlighted
- [[Chick Corea]] — fusion pianist mentioned as example of musician to learn from
- [[Herbie Hancock]] — keyboardist mentioned alongside Chick Corea as master to study

## Concepts Introduced

- Transcription (ear-based learning)
- Learning by feel vs. learning by notation
- The three-step method: listen, sing, play

## Practice Notes

**Three-step transcription method:**
1. Listen 21 times minimum before touching an instrument
2. Sing through the passage to internalize phrasing and feel
3. Take it to the instrument; learn by feel, not just note positions

**Beginner approach:** Pick four-bar sections. Spend 5–10 minutes daily. Transcribe a solo you love. Do not rush into transcribing entire albums.

**What to listen for:** melody, bass line, chord changes (thirds and sevenths), feel and phrasing.

Many musicians own transcription books—famous collections of transcribed solos—yet ask themselves honestly: "How much of those solos have I actually absorbed?" The gap between reading a transcription and living that transcription is vast.

[4:46](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=oRdkepq3ARk&t=286s) [[Robert Glasper]], a contemporary world-class musician, describes his approach: he could not "hit" (play along with or emulate) the records of legendary musicians [[Herbie Hancock]] and [[Chick Corea]] when learning. ### [6:17] The Transcription Process - Three Steps — [▶](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=oRdkepq3ARk&t=377s)

[6:17](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=oRdkepq3ARk&t=377s) Having established the why and what, the instructor breaks the transcription process into three simple but non-negotiable steps.

[6:27](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=oRdkepq3ARk&t=387s) **Step One: Listen 21 times.** This is a specific number. Before the instrument comes out, the transcriber listens repeatedly—21 times minimum. These sessions need not happen in one day; they can be spread over multiple days. The goal is saturation: the ear becomes intimately familiar with every contour, every surprise, every resolution.

[6:54](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=oRdkepq3ARk&t=416s) **Step Two: Sing it.** All of the great musicians sing constantly. One historical example: [[Lester Young]] stopped playing mid-solo because he forgot the words, illustrating how deeply singing is integrated into his concept. Singing internalizes the passage so that the player doesn't have to overthink it. The voice is the direct line to understanding phrasing and feel. Spend a whole day—or multiple days—singing with the recording.

[7:22](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=oRdkepq3ARk&t=442s) **Step Three: Take it to the instrument.** Now, sit down and try to learn from your own singing voice—from that internalization, not from music notation or from the recording directly. See what you can figure out from feel and muscle memory.

[7:42](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=oRdkepq3ARk&t=462s) This takes discipline. The urge to grab the instrument in the first ten minutes is strong. The instructor's recommendation: wait. Let the material sit and absorb for several days. When you finally take it to your instrument, the learning happens at a deeper level. It is not a surface read-through. It is understanding.

[8:06](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=oRdkepq3ARk&t=486s) Concrete listening targets: focus on the melody of the solo, the bass line's movement, the implied thirds and sevenths of the harmonies the bass is playing. All of these are "super helpful." But remember: feel matters more than notes. Walk in the shoes of the masters. Do the work yourself rather than letting a YouTuber transcribe it for you. Get your hands dirty. That is what builds genuine musicianship.

# Secondary dominance

Secondary dominance refers to dominant seventh chords that resolve to non-tonic chords within a key. While a key has only one natural dominant (the V chord resolving to the I), secondary dominants are V chords built a fifth above each diatonic chord, creating a dominant-to-destination relationship. In C major, the secondary dominants are A7→D minor seven, B7→E minor seven, C7→F major seven, D7→G7, E7→A minor seven, and F sharp 7→B minor seven flat five. Secondary dominants appear frequently in jazz standards and Great American Songbook tunes because they add harmonic movement and sophistication while still supporting the underlying diatonic framework. Recognizing secondary dominants—"Oh, that's a secondary dominant to the III chord"—is a game-changing skill that can explain approximately 80% of the harmony in standards. When improvising over secondary dominants, scale choices depend on the resolution destination: when resolving to a major chord, Mixolydian provides a natural unaltered sound; when resolving to a minor chord or dominant chord, the harmonic minor scale of the destination transposed to the dominant root provides more evocative color that acknowledges the harmonic trajectory ahead.
